By isolating the early poetic strata of the <b>Atthakavagga</b> and the <b>Jataka Gatha</b> from later orthodox commentarial traditions (<b>atthakatha</b>), this paper illuminates how early ascetic concepts, secular folklore, and historical identifiers were systematically domesticated, sanitized, and re-contextualized to serve evolving monastic institutional paradigms.</p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><h3 style="text-align: left;">1. Statistical Analysis of Buddhological Titles and Status</h3><div><br /></div><p>The transition from fluid, early ascetic movements to an organized monastic institution is reflected in the sparse occurrence of formal titles within the earliest textual strata. According to data from Bhikkhu Dukil’s doctoral dissertation, "<b>Pre-institutional Buddhist Traditions in the Arthapada</b>" (UCLA, 2024), the term "<b>Buddha</b>" (Awakened One) occurs only once across all 16 suttas (discourses) in the Atthakavagga, which represents the earliest textual stratum or pre-institutional era.</p><p><br /></p><p>This single occurrence appears exclusively in the third verse of the <b>Sariputta Sutta</b>:</p><p><b>"taṃ buddhaṃ asitaṃ tādiṃ / akuhaṃ gaṇimāgataṃ / bahūnamidha baddhānaṃ / atthi pañhena āgamaṃ"</b></p><p>(I have come with a question to that Buddha, who is unattached, unperturbed, deceitless, and the leader of an assembly of disciples who were once bound in this world.)</p><p><br /></p><p>Crucially, this solitary utterance is voiced by the disciple Sariputta as an epithet of respect, rather than a dogmatic self-proclamation or theological declaration by Gotama himself. Furthermore, the frequency of the title "<b>Tathagata</b>" (Thus Gone) is zero within this entire stratum.</p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><h3 style="text-align: left;">2. Lexical Evolution and Conceptual Shifts Samaṇa vs. Bhikkhu</h3><div><br /></div><p>A comparative semantic analysis reveals how the institutionalization of the Sangha (monastic order) fundamentally altered early ascetic terminology between the pre-institutional stratum of the Atthakavagga and the post-institutional stratum of the Four Main Nikayas.</p><p><br /></p><div><b>Regarding the term "Samaṇa" (Recluse)</b>: In the Atthakavagga, the term exhibits a distinct functional duality based on grammatical numbers. When used in the plural form (samaṇā or samaṇesu), it carries an external, sociological, and critical connotation. As seen in texts like the Pasūra Sutta (Sn 4.8) and Cūḷaviyūha Sutta (Sn 4.12), the plural samaṇā reflects the text’s overarching critique of sectarian wanderers and ascetics of the era who were deeply entangled in ideological disputes, dogmatic attachments, and contentions ("ete vivādā samaṇesu jātā").</div><div><br /></div><p>Conversely, when employed in the singular instrumental form (samaṇena), the term transitions into an exalted, positive honorific epithet applied specifically to the Buddha (Gotama Muni) as the proclaimer of truth. In the Kalahavivāda Sutta (Sn 4.11), the singular title denotes the ultimate authority of the true psychological teaching, validating the doctrines expounded by the Buddha ("ye vāpi dhammā samaṇena vuttā" and "ñatvā pavuttā samaṇena dhammā").</p><p><br /></p><p>While the literal compound Mahāsamaṇa (Great Ascetic) is absent from the core verses of the Atthakavagga, this specific positive trajectory of the singular epithet is firmly attested in the canonical main body text of the Vinaya Piṭaka. In the Mahākhandhaka narrative (Sāriputtamoggallānapabbajjākathā), the title is employed directly by Ven. Assaji as a supreme spiritual identifier to define the Buddha’s identity, as preserved in the root text:</p><p>“<b>Atthāvuso, mahāsamaṇo sakyaputto sakyakulā pabbajito, tāhaṁ bhagavantaṁ uddissa pabbajito, so ca me bhagavā satthā, tassa cāhaṁ bhagavato dhammaṁ rocemī”ti.</b></p><p>(Translation: "There is, friend, the Great Ascetic, a son of the Shakyas, gone forth from a Shakya clan; it is on account of that Blessed One that I have gone forth. And that Blessed One is my teacher, and it is the Dhamma of that Blessed One that I approve.")</p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><div><b>For the term "Bhikkhu" (Mendicant)</b>: In the Atthakavagga, rather than serving as a highly rigid, post-institutional legal identity, the term bhikkhu (occurring 16 times) functions primarily as a practical agent and an active practitioner of the path. The term is highly concentrated in Tuvaṭaka Sutta (Sn 4.14) and Sāriputta Sutta (Sn 4.16) to depict a disciple undergoing mental navigation, solitary mindfulness, and active spiritual discipline under the direct guidance of the Buddha's dispensation ("vicinaṃ bhikkhu sadā sato sikkhe... sāsane gotamassa na pamajjeyya").</div><div><br /></div><p>While it centers heavily on internal psychological cultivation, solitary forest meditation, and the eradication of inner clinging, the post-institutional stratum of the Four Main Nikayas later finalizes this role into a highly structured monastic identity, denoting formal membership within an organization governed strictly by the Vinaya code.</p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><h3 style="text-align: left;">3. Cross-Sectarian Title (Arhat / Arahant)</h3><div><br /></div><div>In <b>Jainism</b>, the term <b>Arihanta</b> (Prakrit) or <b>Arhat</b> (Sanskrit) is explicitly used in their fundamental prayer, the <b>Ṇamōkāra Mantra</b>:</div><div><br /></div><div><div>"<b>Ṇamō arihantāṇaṁ</b>"</div><div>(Obeisance to the arihants/arhats).</div></div><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><h3 style="text-align: left;">4. The Psychological Mechanics of "Uprooting the Arrow" (Attadanda Sutta)</h3><div><br /></div><p>In the <b>Attadanda Sutta</b> (Atthakavagga 15), Sakyamuni recounts his own existential crisis prior to his awakening. The narrative is completely devoid of theological mythos or supernatural intervention, presenting a purely phenomenological and psychological framework through several stages:</p><p><br /></p><p><b>First</b>, Samvega (Existential Dread), which is a profound sense of trauma and horror experienced upon witnessing humanity locked in violent conflict, raising weapons against one another.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>Second</b>, the Fish Analogy, where humanity is perceived as gasping in terror, struggling like fish trapped in a shallow, drying pond.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>Third</b>, the Discovery of the Salla (Arrow), which is the critical realization that human suffering is not inflicted by the external world, but by an internal, unseen arrow (salla) that is lodged within the heart (hadayanissita) and exceedingly difficult to perceive.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>Fourth</b>, the Therapeutic Formula, where liberation is achieved by psychological deconditioning and a systematic emptying of the mind: do not grasp or obsess over the past, do not construct speculative anxieties about the future, and do not cling to fixed identities or emotional reifications in the present.</p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><h3 style="text-align: left;">5. Secular Origins and the Domestication of the Jataka Gatha</h3><div><br /></div><p>When the poetic verses of the <b>Jataka Gatha</b> are isolated and stripped of their later prose commentaries (<b>Atthakatha</b>), they reveal a secular, pre-Buddhist substratum consisting of ancient folklore, rustic fables, and socio-political satire. The post-institutional tradition later domesticated these verses into orthodox religious paradigms by overlaying them with elaborate prose framing narratives:</p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><p>In the <b>Munika Jataka (No. 30)</b>, the original gatha (verse) is a simple, secular peasant rhyme warning a hard-working ox about a pampered pig being fattened up for slaughter. The post-institutional prose layer retroactively frames this as a moral lesson for a monk tempted to disrobe for a woman.</p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><p>In the <b>Vālodaka Jataka (No. 183)</b>, the original verse (gāthā) is a social satire contrasting well-bred thoroughbred horses (sindhava) who maintain their dignity after drinking fine liquor, with low-bred donkeys (gadrabha) who become rowdy and tipsy on cheap, fibrous dregs. The commentarial tradition frames this as a cautionary tale told by the Buddha at Jetavana to address a real-world situation where the noble householders behaved impeccably during meals, while their loud and unruly attendants mirrored the disruptive behavior of the drunken donkeys.</p><p><br /></p><p>Applying textual criticism and stratification analysis, it reveals that the original verse contains absolutely no thematic connection to past lives or the mythos of transmigration; it functions purely as a secular social satire or a folk fable addressing contemporary class behavior, which was only retroactively forced into a rebirth framework by later institutional editors.</p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><p>In the <b>Udañcani Jataka (No. 106)</b>, the original verse (gāthā) is a piece of secular, domestic poetry capturing a husband’s grievance over his wife’s endless material demands. The orthodox commentarial tradition (<b>atthavaṇṇanā</b>) re-contextualized and sanitized this everyday complaint into an allegorical cautionary tale, transforming the demanding wife into a seductive village maiden used by an ascetic master to warn his young disciple against the traps of lay life.</p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;"><br /></h2><h3 style="text-align: left;">6. Onomastic Critique: Gotama, Siddhattha, and Sakyamuni</h3><div><br /></div><p>An evaluation of the names attributed to the historical Buddha reveals a chronological progression from hereditary designations to mythologized hagiography.</p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><p>The name "<b>Siddhattha</b>" (Siddhartha) is entirely absent, with zero occurrences across all four primary Nikayas and the Sutta Nipata. It is a late, post-institutional development designed to fulfill the requirements of sacred hagiographies and late biographical narratives.</p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><p>The name "<b>Gotama</b>" is a Brahminical clan name historically borrowed or adopted by the Sakya warrior caste. It appears as a localized identifier, as seen in the Tuvataka Sutta, verse 19:</p><p>"<b>etañca dhammamaññāya / vicinaṃ bhikkhu sadā sato sikkhe / santīti nibbutiṃ ñatvā / sāsane gotamassa na pamajjeyya</b>"</p><p>(Knowing this Dhamma, a mindful mendicant should investigate and train. Understanding peace as extinguishment, one should not neglect the teaching of <b>Gotama</b>.)</p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><p>The title '<b>Sakyamuni</b>' (Sage of the Shakyas) is an early historic honorific epithet firmly validated by both epigraphic evidence and the earliest textual strata. The term is explicitly anchored in concrete antiquity via the Lumbini Pillar Inscription of Emperor Ashoka (c. 249 BCE), where the compound Sakyamuni is carved to authenticate the birth site. Philologically, the component Muni (the Silent/Enlightened Sage) reflects the oldest conceptual layer of the Pali Canon, most notably dominating the Atthakavagga (Sutta Nipata) as a generic description for a solitary, realized ascetic who has transcended philosophical disputations (diṭṭhi). This textual lineage of clan-based and ascetic descriptors is further solidified by the canonical main body text of the Vinaya Piṭaka, specifically within the <b>Mahākhandhaka</b> (<b>Sāriputtamoggallānapabbajjākathā</b>). In this Vinaya stratum, the title is employed directly by Ven. Assaji as a supreme spiritual identifier to define the Buddha's identity, explicitly combining his family lineage with his supreme spiritual status by pairing the identifiers <b>sakyaputto</b> (son of the Shakyas) and <b>sakyakulā</b> (from the Shakya clan) alongside the exalted epithet mahāsamaṇa (Great Ascetic). The title '<b>Sakyamuni</b>' therefore evolved organically as an early historic identifier, effectively marrying the philosophical ideal of the <b>Muni</b> from the Atthakavagga with the verified clan-based lineage (Sakya) formally recognized by both the earliest monastic records and the Mauryan state.</p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><p>In contrast, "<b>Adiccabandhu</b>" (Kinsman of the Sun) represents the most authentic, poetic, and verified designation originating from the pre-institutional layer of the Atthakavagga. It references the Buddha's actual solar dynastic lineage without the heavy ideological baggage of later institutionalized religiosity, as evidenced in the opening verse of the <b>Tuvataka Sutta</b>:</p><p>"<b>pucchāmi taṃ ādiccabandhu / vivekaṃ santipadañca mahesi...</b>"</p><p>(I ask you, Kinsman of the Sun, great seer, about seclusion and the state of peace...)</p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">Conclusion</h2><div><br /></div><p>In conclusion, historical-critical analysis demonstrates that recovering the unmediated meaning of early Buddhist poetry requires bypassing the orthodox commentarial lens. Stripping away the post-institutional apparatus reveals a highly psychological, pre-sectarian core that operates independently of late scholastic dogmas, institutionalized monastic titles, or teleological myths of rebirth. By returning to the archaic strata of the Atthakavagga and the original Jataka verses, scholars can engage with the foundational, existential insights of the historical movement before it was heavily systematicized by later traditions.</p>
