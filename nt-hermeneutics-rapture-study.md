Biblical Terminology: The "Rapture"</b></h2><p><b>Terminology</b>: In English translations, this event is commonly described as being "caught up" or "the catching away of the Church."</p><p><b>Etymology</b>: The English noun "Rapture" originates from the Greek verb Harpazo (meaning to seize, snatch, or carry away by force). In the Latin Vulgate, this was rendered as raptus (from rapere). English theologians later adopted the Latin root to coin the theological term "Rapture."</p><p><b>Key Verse</b>: 1 Thessalonians 4:17 ("Then we who are alive and remain shall be caught up [harpazō / rapemur] together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air...").</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;"><b>2. Eschatological Framework: Rapture vs. Resurrection</b></h2><p><b>Sequence of Events</b>: Both events form a single chronological package. According to 1 Thessalonians 4:16-17, deceased Christians will experience the bodily resurrection first, followed immediately by living Christians being caught up (the Rapture).</p><p><b>The Meaning of "To Meet" (Apantesis)</b>: In ancient Roman culture, the word apantesis referred to citizens going outside the city gates to welcome an arriving king, then turning around to escort the king back into the city. This provides a theological indication that the Church caught up into the air will subsequently descend back to earth with Jesus.</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;"><b>3. Theological Systems: Mid-Acts Dispensationalism and the Marcionite Issue</b></h2><p><b>Mid-Acts Dispensationalism</b>: A theological school of thought that divides the Bible into strict "dispensations" (stewardships/administrations). They believe that Paul’s epistles contain a specific "Mystery" regarding a heavenly program (for the Church/the Body of Christ), which is completely distinct from the earthly program (for Israel/the Jews).</p><p><b>Marcion of Sinope (2nd Century)</b>: Although he held the earliest canon of Pauline epistles (Apostolikon), Marcion did not believe in a literal Rapture or physical resurrection. As an adherent of Gnosticism/Docetism, Marcion viewed the physical body as inherently evil and unsavable; to him, salvation was strictly the liberation of the spirit into the heavenly realm.</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;"><b>4. Polemical Applications: The Charismatic Issue</b></h2><p><b>Definition</b>: A modern Christian movement believing that supernatural spiritual gifts (charismata)—such as tongues, prophecy, and miraculous healing mentioned in Paul's letters (1 Corinthians 12)—remain active and available for the church today.</p><p><b>The Mid-Acts Refutation</b>: Dispensationalists criticize Charismatics for failing to "rightly divide" biblical eras. They argue that these miraculous gifts were merely temporary "signs" for the Jewish nation during Paul’s early transition period and ceased once the Age of Grace matured (noting that Paul's final epistles no longer mention them).</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;"><b>5. The Hermeneutical Dilemma: The "Two Gospels" Controversy (Kingdom vs. Grace)</b></h2><p><b>The Concept Explained</b>: Mid-Acts dispensationalists create a sharp, extreme division between two messages in the New Testament. They argue that Jesus and Peter preached the "Gospel of the Kingdom" (an earthly, law-based message meant exclusively for Israel), while the Apostle Paul later revealed the "Gospel of the Grace of God" (a heavenly, faith-alone message meant exclusively for the non-Jewish Church).</p><p><b>The Core Problem</b>: This extreme separation creates severe textual conflicts, leading everyday readers to believe that Jesus’ own teachings in the Gospels contradict Paul's letters, as if they are two competing religions.</p><p><b>Textual Contradictions</b>: Paul himself frequently uses the term "Kingdom of God" in his epistles (e.g., 1 Cor. 6:9-10, Rom. 14:17) and states that the Church will judge the world/earth. This proves that Grace and Kingdom are not two opposing programs; rather, the Gospel of Grace is simply the means by which a believer enters the Kingdom of God.</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;"><b>6. Historical and Canonical Polemics: Jewish Christians vs. Paul</b></h2><p><b>Paul vs. James</b>: An early theological tension existed regarding the nature of salvation texts. Paul emphasized justification by faith apart from the works of the Law (Romans 3:28), whereas James emphasized that faith without works is dead (James 2:24).</p><p><b>The Ebionite/Nazarene Sects</b>: These early Jewish-Christian communities completely rejected Paul's apostleship. They labeled Paul a "False Apostle," viewing him as someone who abolished the Mosaic Law that Jesus and James upheld.</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;"><b>7. Bibliology: Direct Speech (Torah) vs. Human Commentary (New Testament)</b></h2><p><b>The Uniqueness of the Pentateuch (Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers)</b>: These texts are unique because they are filled with word-for-word "Direct Speech" from God, utilizing the formula "And the LORD spoke...".</p><p><b>The Nature of the New Testament</b>: No New Testament book mirrors the stylistic model of Leviticus. The Gospels function as eyewitness biographies, while Pauline epistles are personal, pastoral human letters reflecting the logic, emotions, and cultural context of the author.</p><p><b>The Case of John 3:16:</b> This serves as a prime example of how textual nature affects interpretation. Because the original Greek manuscripts lack quotation marks, scholars debate whether verse 16 comprises the direct words of Jesus or is simply a commentary/footnote by the Apostle John.</p><p><b>Red-Letter Bibles</b>: This system is a modern innovation (dating back to 1900) and relies entirely on the publisher's theological interpretation. For instance, John 3:16 is printed in red in the NKJV version (classified as Jesus' direct words) but is printed in black in the 2011 NIV version (classified as human commentary).</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;"><b>8. Recommended Research &amp; Homework for Readers</b></h2><p><b>Study</b> the stylistic differences in law delivery between the Book of Leviticus (God's direct dictation) and the Book of Deuteronomy (Moses' recapitulatory sermon).</p><p><b>Examine</b> how the Jewish Scribal (Masoretic) tradition preserved the textual purity of Direct Speech in the Torah compared to the historical books.</p><p><b>Explore</b> the theological concept of Dynamic Inspiration to understand how texts written in the form of human letters can be regarded as sacred scripture.</p>
