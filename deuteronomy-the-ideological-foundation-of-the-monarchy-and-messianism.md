Main Thesis &amp; the Position of the Book of Deuteronomy</b></p><p><b>The Main Axis of the Bible:</b> The Book of Deuteronomy is recognized by biblical scholars as the theological heart that drives the narrative of the subsequent books (the Nebi'im, Ketuvim, all the way to the New Testament).</p><p><b>Foundation of the Messianic Idea:</b> This book serves as the foundational document for the development of the concept of a savior figure (the Messiah) in both Judaism and all Christian sects.</p><p><b>Tool for Political Reform:</b> Based on the Documentary Hypothesis (Source Criticism), Deuteronomy is a product of the Deuteronomist (D) source, functioning as a reform document to transition the tribal nomadic system into a centralized monarchy.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>2. The True Tradition of the Israelites (Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers</b>)</p><p><b>True Nomadic Memory:</b> The books of Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers preserve layers of oral traditions, customs, and ancient laws born out of the practical life of a nomadic society in the wilderness.</p><p><b>Original Worship Institution:</b> In this original tradition, there was no concept of a permanent temple. The only legitimate locus of the divine presence was the mobile Tabernacle or Tent of Meeting (Ohel Moed).</p><p><b>The Beginning of National Law:</b> From a legal standpoint, the Torah (Law) that binds Israel as a nation chronologically spans from the Sinai Event (Exodus) to the Book of Numbers. The Book of Genesis sits outside this legal corpus as it only serves as a prologue/family history.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>3. Redactional Adaptations of the Tribal Blessings in Genesis 49 vs. Deuteronomy 33</b></p><p><b>Redactional Traces (Editing):</b> The Deuteronomist scribal circle is traced to have adapted ancient poetic traditions in Genesis 49 and paralleled them with Deuteronomy 33 to legitimize contemporary political and religious agendas.</p><p><b>The Case of the Tribe of Judah (Genesis 49:10):</b> Literal linguistic analysis shows that the phrase "the scepter shall not depart from Judah" is a post-Mosaic redactional insertion, retroactively integrated to legitimize the hegemony of the Davidic dynasty.</p><p><b>The Case of the Tribe of Levi:</b> In Genesis 49, Levi is portrayed as a tribe cursed and scattered due to their violence. In Deuteronomy 33, their status is completely reversed into a holy priestly guild, an ideological shift necessary to support the centralized theocratic system.</p><p><b>The Case of the Tribe of Simeon:</b> The name of Simeon is completely omitted from the blessings in Deuteronomy 33. Geopolitically, this reflects the historical reality where the small tribe had already been assimilated and absorbed by the dominant tribe of Judah.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>4. The Character of the Law of Circumcision in Leviticus</b></p><p><b>Exclusion from the Holiness Code:</b> The command for circumcision is entirely absent from the Holiness Code (Leviticus 17–26). Instead, it is situated within the ritual purity laws in Leviticus 12:3, which addresses the purification of a mother after childbirth.</p><p><b>Gender Separation via Pronouns:</b> Literal linguistic analysis of Leviticus 12:3 shows that the text employs a specific masculine singular pronominal suffix (the -ô ending in 'orlato), meaning "his foreskin".</p><p><b>Exclusivity of the Law:</b> This explicit use of masculine grammar establishes that the mandate applies strictly to male infants on the eighth day, leaving absolutely no textual or legal framework for female circumcision within the Torah.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>5. The Evolution of the Centralized Temple Concept</b></p><p><b>Conceptual Shift:</b> The Deuteronomist source (Deuteronomy 12) began introducing the theological idea of "the single fixed place that will be chosen" to replace the Ohel Moed system.</p><p><b>Historical Realization:</b> This permanent stone temple idea was physically executed by King Solomon and clearly recorded in the Book of 1 Kings chapters 6 &amp; 8, utilizing theological language that mirrors the blueprint of the Book of Deuteronomy.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>6. The Evolution of Cultic Worship Traditions</b></p><p><b>Altering Nomadic Traditions:</b> Deuteronomy topologically and theologically alters the original nomadic tradition (Exodus–Numbers) centered around the mobile Tent of Meeting (Ohel Moed).</p><p><b>Single Ritual Center (Cultic Centralization)</b>: Through the repetition of the command "the place that will be chosen" (Deuteronomy 12), the book lays the groundwork for the centralization of cultic worship.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>7. Impact on Two Modern Religions:</b></p><p><b>Judaism:</b> Interprets this principle of centralization as absolute legitimacy for building a single Temple in Jerusalem, which eventually evolved into a text-and-book-based religion (Rabbinic Judaism) after the temple was destroyed.</p><p><b>Christianity:</b> Anchors its theology in the prophecy of a future leader in Deuteronomy 18:15 (a Prophet like Moses) to justify and validate the messianic mission of Jesus as recorded by the authors of the New Testament.</p>
