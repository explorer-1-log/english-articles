In this book, Al-Ghazali raises <b>20 critical questions</b> to dismantle the arguments of earlier Muslim philosophers—such as Ibn Sina (Avicenna) and Al-Farabi—who were heavily influenced by Greek thought. In fact, on three core issues (Questions 1, 2, and 20), Al-Ghazali goes so far as to declare these philosophers apostates.</p><p><br /></p><p>However, if we re-examine this historical debate through a pure Quranic linguistic and epistemological lens, we uncover a startling reality: <b>Both sides—the philosophers and the theologian (Al-Ghazali)—committed a fundamental methodological error</b>. They trapped themselves in the same "prison of terminology" and forced foreign frameworks of thought onto the divine text, even though the Quran itself is entirely free from such flaws.</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">1. The Axial Age Trap: Why Philosophical Structures Were Born</h2><p>To understand the root of this conflict, we must look back to the <b>Axial Age</b> (around 800–200 BCE). During this era, global civilizations in Greece (Socrates, Plato, Aristotle) and India (the Upanishadic and Abhidhamma traditions) underwent a massive intellectual revolution. Humans began <b>dissecting reality into abstract components</b> to understand the world.</p><p>It was from this Axial Age framework that reality was divided into:</p><p><b>Substance (Jawhar)</b>: The primary, enduring physical entity (e.g., a human body, a chariot, or water).</p><p><b>Accident ('Aradh)</b>: External properties that depend on a substance and can change without destroying the entity's core nature (e.g., paint color, speed, or temperature).</p><p><br /></p><p>Meanwhile, in India, <b>Classical Theravada Abhidhamma</b> theory divided the world into the smallest possible components called Dhammas. In this view, elemental properties (such as the heat of fire or the hardness of earth) were elevated as <b>Sabhava (intrinsic nature/the reality of the natural world)</b> itself, rather than just dependent accidents.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>Where did the problem lie?</b> Muslim philosophers attempted to take these Axial Age formulas of elemental components and force them onto the Essence and Attributes of God.</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">2. Distorting Terminology: The Robotic God vs. The Quranic God</h2><p>In <b>Questions 1 through 13</b>, the debate centers heavily on the structure of the Cosmos, Existence, and God’s Knowledge.</p><p>Ibn Sina faced an intellectual dilemma. He wanted to preserve Greek philosophy while remaining aligned with Islam. To bridge this, he resorted to syncresis (forced integration). He took Plato’s concept of <b>The Good</b>—the highest ideal principle at the peak of the Realm of Forms—and conflated it with Plotinus’s Neo-Platonic concept of <b>The One</b>. He then combined this with Aristotle’s <b>Unmoved Mover</b>.</p><p>This synthesis gave birth to the <b>Theory of Emanation (Cosmic Radiance)</b>. Ibn Sina claimed that the cosmos flows out of The One (God) automatically, mechanically, and eternally without a beginning in time (qadim), much like light radiating from the sun. To preserve God's immutability, Ibn Sina argued that God cannot have attributes distinct from His Essence (Question 6). He also claimed it was impossible for God to directly know the fluid details or particulars (juz'iyyat) of human life (Question 13), because changing human events would imply a change in God's knowledge.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>Al-Ghazali’s Trap</b>:</p><p>Al-Ghazali fiercely attacked Ibn Sina to defend the faith (Questions 3, 4, 5). However, the ultimate tragedy is that <b>Al-Ghazali accepted Ibn Sina’s playing field and language</b>. He spent immense intellectual energy debating the abstract "Cosmos/Universe," "Accidents," and "Substances"—concepts the Quran itself never discusses! Al-Ghazali was forced to build complex, speculative theological formulas just to counter Ibn Sina’s syllogisms.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>The Liberation of the Quran</b>:</p><p>The Quran never conceptualizes the universe through an abstract, independent philosophical lens (such as the Greek Cosmos or the later scholastic Al-Kawn). While the Quran does employ the term Al-`Alamin (العالمين), its semantic scope is strictly binary and relational, depending entirely on the textual context. It never functions as a static, standalone physical universe.</p><p>Instead, whenever the text addresses the ultimate totality of creation, Al-'Alamin shrinks or expands into two definitive physical boundaries: either the macroscopic scope of '<b>the heavens, the earth, and whatever is between them</b>' (as defined by Moses in 26:24), or the domestic scope of '<b>human societies and historical civilizations</b>' (as seen in 29:28). By grounding Al-'Alamin within these specific relational boundaries, the Quranic framework refuses to look at the universe as a cold, self-sustaining mechanical object.</p><p>The Quran has no interest in the existential riddles of the Axial Age (whether something exists, does not exist, both, or neither). It is completely free from that Catuskoti (Tetralemma). In the Quran, the existence of God is a given truth (self-evident). The Quran introduces God through language that is <b>Functional (what He does)</b> and <b>Relational (how He relates to humanity)</b>. Allah is the active Ruler of the heavens and the earth Who hears prayers, sees actions, and governs His creation at every moment through His Absolute Will (Iradah), rather than a cold, mechanical first cause.</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">Summary of Key Perspectives</h2><div><br /></div><p><b>The Philosophers (Ibn Sina)</b></p><p>Core Influence: Greek Philosophy (Plato, Aristotle, Plotinus).</p><p>Concept of God: Mechanical and static; an automatic source of emanation.</p><p>View of the Universe: An eternal, abstract "Cosmos".</p><p><br /></p><p><b>The Theologian (Al-Ghazali)</b></p><p>Core Influence: Abstract Philosophical Counter-Arguments.</p><p>Concept of God: Defined through complex scholastic theology (Kalam).</p><p>View of the Universe: A composite of "Substances" and "Accidents".</p><p><br /></p><p><b>The Quranic Framework</b></p><p>Core Influence: Pure Divine Revelation.</p><p>Concept of God: Active and personal; driven by Absolute Will (Iradah).</p><p>View of the Universe:&nbsp;A relational structure (Al-`Alamin) that dynamically expands or shrinks based on context: either encompassing the heavens, the earth, and all between them (macroscopic) or narrowing strictly to human civilizations on earth (domestic).</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">3. The Misunderstanding of the Terms Nafs and 'Aql</h2><p>In <b>Questions 14 through 19</b>, the debate shifts toward celestial cosmology and the anatomy of the human soul. Ibn Sina claimed that planets possess a Soul (Nafs) and an Intellect ('Aql) that move voluntarily out of a deep yearning for God (Questions 14 &amp; 15). He also asserted that the human soul is an immaterial, incorporeal <b>spiritual substance (soul/psyche)</b> imprisoned within the physical body—mirroring Plato's Phaedo dialogue—and is inherently immortal (Questions 18 &amp; 19).</p><p><br /></p><p><b>The Quranic Semantic Deconstruction</b>:</p><p>When we carefully examine the pure text of the Quran, we find that the philosophers committed a major linguistic error:</p><p>1. <b>'Aql (Intellect/Reason)</b>: In the Quran, <b>the word 'aql never appears as a noun</b>. Instead, the Quran consistently uses it as a verb, such as ta'qilun (you reason) or ya'qilun (they understand). This signifies that intellect in the Quranic worldview is not a mental organ, an anatomical component, or a cosmic entity; rather, it is <b>an active process (the activity of thinking and understanding)</b>. Planets do not engage in this process!</p><p>2. <b>Nafs (Soul/Self)</b>: In Quranic Arabic, nafs is not some mystical spiritual gas or vapor that enters and leaves the body in the Greek tradition. Rather, nafs literally means the <b>Existential Identity of an Entity (its "self" or "being")</b>. Trees, rocks, the heavens, and lifeless idols all possess a nafs—their own existential identity [7:197]. The only difference lies in the <b>level of accountability (moral and legal responsibility)</b>, which Allah places exclusively on humans and jinn who possess free will [74:38].</p><p><br /></p><p>Consequently, the debates between Al-Ghazali and Ibn Sina regarding "celestial souls" or the "immortality of the spiritual substance" were essentially self-indulgent academic exercises. The very subjects they were debating were built upon external terminology that simply does not exist within the linguistic structure of divine revelation.</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">Summary of Conceptual Shifts</h2><p><b><br /></b></p><p><b>'Aql (Intellect)</b></p><p>Philosophical Interpretation: An anatomical component, mental organ, or cosmic entity capable of independent existence.</p><p>Quranic Semantic Framework: An active process (verb); the conscious act of reasoning, thinking, and understanding.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>Nafs (Soul/Self)</b></p><p>Philosophical Interpretation: An immaterial spiritual substance (psyche) trapped inside a physical body.</p><p>Quranic Semantic Framework: Existential Identity (noun); the essence of an entity's being, ranging from objects to morally accountable creation.</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">4. The Pinnacle of Controversy: Miracles and Physical Resurrection</h2><p>This clash of mechanical frameworks reaches its absolute peak in <b>Question 17 (Miracles)</b> and <b>Question 20 (The Resurrection in the Hereafter)</b>.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>Ibn Sina</b>: Bound by the parameters of mechanical physics, he argued that cause-and-effect (causality) is mandatory and absolute. Fire must burn. Consequently, he rejected physical miracles (such as Prophet Abraham not burning in the fire) and denied the bodily resurrection in the afterlife. To him, heaven and hell were merely metaphors for spiritual experiences, as he believed it was physically impossible under the laws of matter for a decomposed body to be reassembled.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>Al-Ghazali</b>: To defend the faith, Al-Ghazali was forced to construct the theory of Occasionalism—claiming that the laws of nature do not exist and that fire does not actually possess the power to burn. Instead, he argued that Allah merely creates the effect of burning on a case-by-case basis. This argument swung to the opposite extreme and risked undermining the very foundations of scientific observation (leading to skepticism). In Question 20, Al-Ghazali busied himself explaining the mechanics of how Allah reassembles the old particles of a body like a jigsaw puzzle.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>The Definitive Quranic Answer</b>:</p><p>The Quran neatly cuts through all the anxieties of ancient science and component-based scholastic theology.</p><p>In the case of Prophet Abraham being saved from the fire (<b>Surah Al-Anbiya' 21:69</b>), the Quran makes absolutely no mention of altering physical properties, substances, or the "accidents" of fire. The Quran simply presents the language of a <b>Direct Command (Amr)</b>: "O fire, be coolness and safety upon Abraham." Fire, as an entity (Nafs) with its own existential identity, simply hears and obeys the command of its Creator.</p><p><br /></p><p>In the case of the bodily resurrection, the Quran addresses the issue decisively in <b>Surah Al-Isra' 17:50-51</b>:</p><p>"Say, 'Be you stones or iron, or any creation of that which is yet greater in your minds [and harder to resurrect].' Then they will say, 'Who will restore us?' Say, 'He who created you the first time.' "</p><p><br /></p><p>The Quran firmly asserts: Resurrection has nothing to do with the physical state of your remains! Whether your body decomposes into dust or transforms into the hardest, most scientifically impossible elements like stone or iron, it does not hinder the Day of Judgment. The Quran does not respond using theories of atomic reassembly; rather, it uses a functional argument:&nbsp;Since Allah created your Existential Identity (Nafs) for the first time, restoring it a second time is incredibly effortless.</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">Summary of Views on Miracles and Resurrection</h2><p><br /></p><p><b>Causality &amp; Miracles</b></p><p>Ibn Sina: Fixed and unalterable; physical miracles are impossible because cause-and-effect cannot break.</p><p>Al-Ghazali: Non-existent laws; nature has no inherent power, and events only occur through continuous, case-by-case divine creation.</p><p>Quranic Framework: Driven by Direct Command (Amr); creation is an existential identity (Nafs) that naturally obeys its Creator.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>Resurrection</b></p><p>Ibn Sina: Purely spiritual; a destroyed physical body cannot be reconstructed under the laws of matter.</p><p>Al-Ghazali: Mechanical reassembly; Allah gathers and pieces the old physical particles back together like a puzzle.</p><p>Quranic Framework:&nbsp;Functional and absolute; independent of material status (even if turned to stone or iron) because He who created identity the first time easily restores it.</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">Conclusion</h2><p>The book Tahafut al-Falasifah stands as historic proof of the magnificent intellectual struggles of classical Islam. However, it also serves as a profound lesson for us today. When we attempt to defend the Quran by borrowing foreign frameworks of thought—whether it is the ancient Greek philosophy of the Axial Age or the modern materialistic scientific paradigms of today—we inadvertently confine the vastness of divine revelation within a prison of human terminology. The Quran is at its most beautiful, decisive, and unshakeable when it is allowed to speak through its own unique linguistic structure: organic, holistic, functional, and relational.</p><p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">Final Takeaways</h2><div><br /></div><p><b>Human-Made Philosophical Systems</b></p><p>Approach: Dissects reality into rigid, abstract, and speculative components.</p><p>Language: Trapped in human-made terms like Substance, Accident, or Cosmos.</p><p>End Result: Leads to intellectual paralysis, skepticism, or a robotic theology.</p><p><br /></p><p><b>The Quranic Linguistic Model</b></p><p>Approach: Presents reality through dynamic, concrete, and relational frameworks.</p><p>Language: Uses functional and universal phrasing like Heavens, Earth, or Self/Identity.</p><p>End Result: Establishes a natural, self-evident truth centered on absolute Divine Will.</p>
