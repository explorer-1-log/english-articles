<h2 style="text-align: left;">1. Introduction</h2>
<p>The study of apocalyptic literature across Abrahamic traditions has frequently been dominated by futuristic readings that interpret images of cosmic destruction as literal predictions of the physical end of the universe in the distant future. However, developments in literary and historical-textual criticism—particularly through the methodologies of Audience Relevance, Time Statements, and Typology commonly examined in first-century Semitic apocalyptic studies—offer an alternative analytical lens rooted more deeply in the original historical context of the text.</p><p><br />This research note aims to apply these hermeneutical tools to the Qur'anic corpus, specifically within the surahs that are heavily laden with warning rhetoric and eschatological imagery. By examining how the text interacted directly with its original seventh-century audience in the Hijaz, this study seeks to uncover the layer of imminent historical fulfillment and literary function behind the Qur'an's apocalyptic metaphors, prior to the comprehensive development of classical eschatological dogmas centuries later.</p><p><br /></p>
<h2 style="text-align: left;">2. Audience Relevance</h2>
<p>A foundational principle in literary and historical-textual criticism is that a message cannot hold a valid meaning for modern readers if that message was completely unintelligible or irrelevant to its original hearers.</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Context of the Original Audience (Seventh-Century Hijazi Society)</span></b>: The apocalyptic language in the Qur'an was not revealed in a vacuum. It was delivered directly to merchants and tribal elites who were deeply familiar with classical Arabic poetic traditions as well as the history of fallen civilizations across the Arabian Peninsula.</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Evidence of Local Physical Ruins</span></b>: The Qur'an repeatedly links threats of retribution to tangible physical ruins that caravans passed along the trade routes to Syria and Yemen, such as the lands of 'Ad, Thamud, Midian, and the people of Lot (for example, in Surah As-Saffat 37:137–138 and Al-Hijr 15:75–76).</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• The Literary Function of Warning</span></b>: When the Qur'an describes the sky splitting apart or the earth shaking, the original audience did not read it as a modern astrophysical report, but rather as the highest form of rhetorical warning—signaling that the social, economic, and power structures they prided themselves on would collapse if they continued to reject the message.</p><p><br /></p>
<h2 style="text-align: left;">3. Time Statements</h2>
<p>One of the most prominent features of the Qur'an's warning rhetoric is the use of temporal markers indicating that the promised event was imminent and fell within the lifespan of the original generation of hearers.</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Use of Verbs and Adjectives of Nearness</span></b>: The Qur'an consistently employs terms such as iqtaraba (has drawn near) and qarib (near). Examples include Surah Al-Qamar 54:1 ("The Hour has drawn near") and Surah Al-Anbiya' 21:1 ("The time of reckoning for the people has drawn near").</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Comparison of Time Perception</span></b>: In Surah Al-Ma'arij 70:6–7, it is stated that while humans view the event as distant, God sees it as near. This highlights a real temporal tension within the consciousness of that community at the time.</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Suddenness (Baghtatan)</span></b>: Surah Al-A'raf 7:187 states that the event will not come upon them except suddenly (baghtatan), while Surah An-Naml 27:72 notes that part of the punishment they sought to hasten was already close behind them (radifa lakum).</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Lifespan Boundaries of the Original Generation</span></b>: Verses such as Surah Yunus 10:46, Ar-Ra'd 13:40, and Ghafir 40:77 explicitly state that Prophet Muhammad would be shown part of what was promised to his opponents within his own lifetime.</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Skeptical Reaction of the Opponents</span></b>: The cynical question repeatedly posed by the associators (<i>Mushrikun</i>) in the Qur'an—"When will this promise be fulfilled, if you are truthful?" (as seen in Surah Yasin 36:48 and Al-Mulk 67:25)—provides textual proof that the original audience understood the promise as an immediate threat to themselves, rather than a prediction for generations thousands of years in the future.</p><p><br /></p>
<h2 style="text-align: left;">4. Historical Typology in the Qur'an</h2>
<p>Within the Qur'an, typology operates through the concept of Sunnatullah (the consistent pattern of God's decrees in history). The accounts of earlier nations function as foreshadows or initial patterns (types), while the events unfolding around Prophet Muhammad and the Hijazi society act as contemporary fulfillments (antitypes).</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• The Typology of Pharaoh and Egypt</span></b>: The Qur'an explicitly parallels the sending of Prophet Muhammad to his people with the sending of Prophet Moses to Pharaoh in Surah Al-Muzzammil 73:15. The ruling elite are positioned typologically as the arrogant Pharaoh, while the small group of the Prophet's followers are placed in the position of the oppressed Children of Israel.</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• The Typology of the Battle of Badr as Yaum al-Furqan</span></b>: The Battle of Badr is named in Surah Al-Anfal 8:41 as Yaum al-Furqan (The Day of Criterion/Distinction). Typologically, it echoes the parting of the Red Sea—a historical turning point in which an oppressed group is decisively delivered and an arrogant, oppressive power structure (the ruling elite) is shattered in a defining confrontation.</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• The Typology of Destroyed Cities (Al-Qura)</span></b>: Whenever the Qur'an references the destruction of earlier towns (such as in Surah Al-Isra' 17:16 and Al-Qasas 28:58–59), it functions as a typological warning that their city is not immune to the same historical law if it imitates the conduct of those past cities.</p><p><br /></p>
<h2 style="text-align: left;">5. Semitic Apocalyptic Metaphors as the Language of Collapsing Power</h2>
<p>The cosmic language found in the short surahs (such as At-Takwir, Al-Infitar, Al-Qari'ah, and Al-Insyiqaq) shares ancient Semitic literary idioms and classical Arabic poetic conventions that symbolize the downfall of political systems and worldly authority.</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Rolled-Up Skies and Falling Stars</span></b>: In Semitic literary traditions and pre-Islamic (Jahiliyah) Arabic poetry, the sky symbolizes sovereignty or the canopy of supreme authority, while stars symbolize chieftains, kings, or tribal leaders. Consequently, expressions such as stars scattering and falling (Surah At-Takwir 81:2) and the sky being rolled up like a scroll (Surah Al-Anbiya' 21:104) carry the figurative meaning of elite leadership collapsing and old sovereignty vanishing.</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Shattered and Moving Mountains</span></b>: In classical Arabic proverbs, mountains represent entities that are firm, permanent, and impossible to topple (such as the ruling elite's economic and religious monopoly). When the Qur'an depicts mountains being set in motion or becoming like carded wool (Surah At-Takwir 81:3 and Al-Qari'ah 101:5), it portrays the absolute collapse of institutions long deemed invulnerable.</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Darkening of the Sun and Moon</span></b>: The dimming of the sun and moon (Surah At-Takwir 81:1 and Al-Qiyamah 75:8–9) is a classic Ancient Near Eastern metaphor referring to the end of a dynasty's golden era or the downfall of a religious system that served as the central axis of society at the time.</p><p><br /></p>
<h2 style="text-align: left;">6. Conclusion</h2>
<p>Overall, examining Qur'anic apocalyptic texts through the combined frameworks of Audience Relevance, Time Statements, and Historical Typology reveals that the eschatological discourse in early revelations possessed an urgent historical and literary function. Cosmic imagery—such as splitting skies, falling stars, and shattered mountains—was not merely a literal report of the physical annihilation of the universe at the end of time, but rather a potent Semitic metaphorical language used to proclaim the collapse of the old socio-political and religious order in the Hijaz.</p><p><br />This approach demonstrates that by respecting the seventh-century original audience context and historical typological patterns (Sunnatullah), the tensions often arising from reading imminent time statements can be resolved consistently. Ultimately, comparative study between ancient Semitic apocalyptic idioms and Qur'anic rhetoric not only enriches our textual understanding of the history of revelation, but also opens new methodological avenues for distinguishing the original literary-historical meaning of the text from the theological dogmas that developed in subsequent eras.</p>
