<h2>Introduction</h2>
<p>Modern Christian eschatological debates are typically divided between the Futurist and Preterist camps regarding the timeline of New Testament prophecy fulfillment. However, both positions actually operate within the exact same textual framework—namely, the Southern theological tradition (Judah and Jerusalem). When placed alongside the historical tradition of the Samaritan Israelites in the North, the entire premise of the Preterist-Futurist debate reveals itself to be exclusive to the Southern narrative.</p>
<h2>1. The Gospels (The Olivet Discourse &amp; Messianic Mission)</h2>
<p><br /><strong>Futurist View</strong>: Interprets Jesus' warnings of great tribulation and the coming of the Son of Man as global, end-of-the-world events occurring in the future.</p>
<p><br /><strong>Preterist View</strong>: Asserts that all of these events were completely fulfilled within "this generation" (the first century), culminating in the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 AD.</p>
<p><br /><strong>Samaritan Israelite Contrast</strong>: The entire discourse surrounding a Davidic Messiah and the fate of Jerusalem holds no theological value for the Samaritan community. They adhere strictly to the Pentateuch (the five books of Moses) with Mount Gerizim as their center of worship, recognizing neither the Davidic monarchy nor the sanctity of Jerusalem.</p>
<h2>2. Paul's Epistles (The Mosaic Law &amp; Ingathering of the Northern Tribes)</h2>
<p><br /><strong>Futurist View</strong>: Reads Paul's theology regarding liberation from the "curse of the Law" and the unification of the nations as a universal doctrine of salvation for modern humanity.</p>
<p><br /><strong>Preterist View</strong>: Views Paul's letters as first-century transitional documents aimed at regathering the scattered descendants of Northern Israel (Ephraim) across the Greco-Roman world prior to the closing of the Old Covenant age.</p>
<p><br /><strong>Samaritan Israelite Contrast</strong>: The concept of a "curse of the Law" and the need for redemption through the blood of a Southern Messiah never arises within the Samaritan framework. The descendants of the Northern tribes (Ephraim and Manasseh) who remained in their homeland continuously preserved the Pentateuch without ever viewing themselves as severed from the covenant, rendering Paul's theology of reunification entirely irrelevant to them.</p>
<h2>3. The Book of Revelation &amp; the Events of 70 AD</h2>
<p><br /><strong>Futurist View</strong>: Interprets the symbolism of Revelation (such as Babylon, the Beast, and the New Heavens) as the collapse of global civilization at the end of time.</p>
<p><br /><strong>Preterist View</strong>: Sees Revelation as a depiction of the fall of Jerusalem and the destruction of the Temple in 70 AD, which permanently ended the Old Covenant sacrificial system and bound Satan's power.</p>
<p><strong>Samaritan Israelite Contrast</strong>: The fall of the Jerusalem Temple in 70 AD does not constitute an "end of the age" for the Samaritans. Because they reject the entire Nevi'im anda Ketuvim canons (the Southern prophetic and apocalyptic writings that form the symbolic foundation of Revelation), the destruction of the rival worship center in Jerusalem is viewed merely as an ordinary political-historical event belonging to the Southern kingdom.</p>
<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>The clash between Preterists and Futurists is ultimately an internal dispute over how to interpret the religious literature of the Southern tradition. The continuous existence of the Samaritan Israelites alongside their Pentateuch demonstrates that issues of law fulfillment, Pauline theology, and the tragedy of 70 AD are neither a universal human narrative nor binding upon the Northern Israelite tradition itself.<br /><br /></p>
