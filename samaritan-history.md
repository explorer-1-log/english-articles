<p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">Introduction</h2><p></p>
<p><br />Discussions surrounding the history and theology of the Israelites have long been dominated by the perspective of the Southern Kingdom of Judah, which places Jerusalem, the Davidic Dynasty, and the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 AD as the ultimate climax of scriptural narrative. However, when this framework is re-examined through the original text of the Torah (Pentateuch) and compared with the perspective of the Northern Kingdom through the Samaritan community at Mount Gerizim, profound questions emerge regarding the accuracy of prophetic timelines, the true original center of worship, and the continuity of purity laws handed down since the time of Moses.</p>
<p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">1. Prophetic Timelines and the Dominance of the Southern Perspective</h2><p></p>
<p><br />In the eschatological interpretation of the Gospels and the Old Testament, the Preterist approach offers the most linguistically consistent logic by understanding the phrase "this generation" as referring directly to the first-century generation that witnessed the fall of Jerusalem in 70 AD. Nevertheless, elevating the fall of Jerusalem as the absolute culmination of God's covenantal history holds true only through a Southern lens. For the Northern Israelite community (the Samaritans)—who recognize only the five books of the Torah and reject the Nevi'im and Ketuvim compiled in the South—Jerusalem has never been their religious focal point, and its destruction was merely a local political event concerning the tribe of Judah.</p>
<p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">2. Ohel Moed vs. Geographical Location: Gerizim and the Altar</h2><p></p>
<p><br />When examined purely through&nbsp; the books of Exodus and Leviticus, the true center of God’s dwelling (Mishkan) among the Israelites was never a stone building or a fixed geographical coordinate, but rather the portable Tabernacle (Ohel Moed). Yet, as the law transitioned into the phase of settlement in the land of Canaan as outlined in Deuteronomy and the Samaritan Torah, the claim of Mount Gerizim in the North emerges as far more organic than that of Jerusalem. Mount Gerizim and the region of Shechem hold direct historical ties to Abraham, Jacob, and the explicit command in the Samaritan Torah to build an altar of covenant stones, whereas Jerusalem remained a foreign Jebusite city until it was conquered by David centuries later.</p>
<p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">3. Purity Laws, the Absence of the Ohel Moed, and the Passover Sacrifice</h2><p></p>
<p><br />The physical absence of the Ohel Moed and the Ark of the Covenant automatically halts almost the entire blood-sacrifice system of Leviticus—including the annual Yom Kippur ritual inside the Holy of Holies and the preparation of the Red Heifer (Parah Adumah) ashes required to cleanse corpse impurity—in accordance with its own legal mechanisms. Under these circumstances, the Samaritan community has maintained a practical approach rooted strictly in the written text without oral legal additions: they suspended the Levitical sacrificial system, actively preserved the ritual purity of their priesthood from physical contact with the dead, and continued only the Passover (Pesach) sacrifice on Mount Gerizim based on the specific provision in Deuteronomy commemorating the deliverance from Egypt.</p>
<p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">4. The Division of the Tribe of Levi and the Priesthood (Uzzi vs. Eli)</h2><p></p>
<p><br />Because the Tribe of Levi possessed no territorial inheritance of its own and was dispersed across cities throughout all Israelite territories, the North-South divide naturally split the priestly lineage into two branches. According to Samaritan historical chronicles, the most critical schism occurred much earlier during the era of the Judges, when Eli (from the secondary branch of Ithamar) broke away from the legitimate High Priest, Uzzi (from the primary lineage of Eleazar and Phinehas), to establish a rival sanctuary at Shiloh. This schism led to the hiding of the original Ohel Moed on Mount Gerizim and ushered in the era of divine concealment (Fanuta), explaining why the Samaritan priestly narrative aligns far more closely with the Torah’s promise of a perpetual priesthood to the descendants of Phinehas.</p>
<p><br /></p><h2 style="text-align: left;">Conclusion</h2><p></p>
<p><br />In conclusion, when Israelite history and law are evaluated directly by the standard of the Torah without the influence of royal political redaction from the South, the Samaritan tradition at Mount Gerizim demonstrates a far more logical and consistent continuity in geography, priestly lineage, and legal observance. Reintegrating this Northern voice proves that Rabbinic Judaism and Christianity do not represent the full picture of Israelite history, but are rather theological evolutions stemming from a single Southern branch.</p>
