<h2>1. Textual Stratification (A Historical-Critical Perspective)</h2>
<p>The Buddhist Sutta Canon available today is a composite text consisting of multiple historical layers. The core, earliest layer comprises ancient poetry, which was later expanded with prose layers containing cosmology, mythology, and monastic bureaucracy designed for the institutional dissemination of the religion.</p>

<!-- SEKSYEN 2 (H2) -->
<h2>2. The Historical Status of the Aṭṭhakavagga</h2>
<p>The Aṭṭhakavagga represents Strata 1 (the oldest layer) within the entire contemporary Buddhist Canon.</p>
<p><b>Organic Poetry</b>: It existed as organic poetry (Gāthā) long before the lengthy prose texts in the Saṁyutta Nikāya or Dīgha Nikāya were bureaucratically composed and compiled.</p>
<p><b>Historical Validation</b>: Other Nikāyas explicitly recognize the Aṭṭhakavagga, recording instances where these sacred verses were recited directly in front of the Buddha during his lifetime.</p>

<!-- SEKSYEN 3 (H2) -->
<h2>3. The Absence of Popular Cosmology and Structural Theology</h2>
<p>The Aṭṭhakavagga represents a form of Pre-Canonical Buddhism that focuses purely on immediate psychological peace rather than dogma. It is uniquely characterized by the complete absence of later institutionalized concepts:</p>
<p><b>The Concept of Saṁsāra</b>: The word Saṁsāra does not exist in the original Aṭṭhakavagga. Human suffering is framed through immediate psychological terms such as Ogha (the flood of emotion/craving) and Saṅga (bonds/attachments), rather than abstract theories of cosmic rebirth.</p>
<p><b>The Four Stages of Enlightenment</b>: The hierarchical grading of spiritual attainment (Sotāpanna, Sakadāgāmī, Anāgāmī, Arahat) is completely absent. The text presents a binary, black-and-white evaluation: you are either trapped in the flood (Ogha) or you have safely crossed over to become a Muni (a silent, wise sage).</p>

<!-- SEKSYEN 4 (H2) -->
<h2>4. The Absence of Formal Doctrinal Formulas</h2>
<p><b>The Four Noble Truths &amp; The Eightfold Path</b>: The Aṭṭhakavagga lacks these systematic formulations. The path to liberation is described practically: maintaining constant mindfulness (Sato), living in seclusion within the forest, and relinquishing greed.</p>
<p><b>The Issue of Anatta (Non-Self)</b>: There are no theoretical formulas such as "Rūpaṁ anattā..." (Form is non-self). Instead, it employs a direct therapeutic approach: a strict prohibition against building an ego based on possessions or knowledge, and the systematic letting go of selfishness.</p>

<!-- SEKSYEN 5 (H2) -->
<h2>5. The Absence of Rigid Mental Classifications (Jhāna &amp; Āyatana)</h2>
<p><b>The 12 Sense Bases (Āyatana)</b>: This technical classification does not exist here. The text relies on ordinary cognitive terms such as Phassa (sensory contact), Saññā (perception), and the experiential processing of what is seen (Diṭṭha), heard (Suta), and sensed (Muta).</p>
<p><b>The Four Formless Meditations (Arūpa-āyatana)</b>: Rigid terms like Nevasaññānāsaññāyatana are absent. Instead, we find the dynamic, original poetic phrase: "Na saññasaññī na visaññasaññī..." (Not of normal perception, yet not insane...). Later editors froze this fluid, experiential description into a rigid technical meditative state.</p>
<p><b>The Term Ākiñcañña</b>: In this text, the term carries a literal, organic meaning—"owning absolutely nothing in this world." It does not refer to the abstract meditative realm of Nothingness found in later texts.</p>

<!-- SEKSYEN 6 (H2) -->
<h2>6. A Radical Stance on "Views" (Diṭṭhi)</h2>
<p>Remarkably, the Aṭṭhakavagga does not offer "Right View" (Sammā Diṭṭhi) as a dogma to be debated. The text asserts that clinging fanatically to any philosophical view or belief system (Diṭṭhi)—by claiming "I am right, you are wrong"—is the primary source of internal bondage. True liberation (Mutto) requires releasing one's grip on all doctrines.</p>

<!-- SEKSYEN 7 (H2) -->
<h2>7. Internal Contradictions in Prose Suttas vs. The Clarity of the Aṭṭhakavagga</h2>
<p>Understanding the raw nature of the Aṭṭhakavagga helps expose the structural and doctrinal contradictions that later emerged within the prose suttas themselves.</p>
<p>A clear example is the issue of consciousness (Viññāṇa). In certain prose texts like the Mahātaṇhāsaṅkhaya Sutta (MN 38), consciousness is strictly described as dependently co-arisen (paṭiccasamuppanna)—it arises and passes away based on immediate conditions, and does not travel or transmigrate. However, this directly contradicts other prose suttas that introduce concepts like antarā-parinibbāyī (attaining Nirvana in the interim) or okkanti (the descending of consciousness into the womb), which implicitly treat consciousness as a moving, traveling entity.</p>
<p>While later scholastic commentaries tried to resolve this internal friction by claiming the suttas merely used conventional language (sammuti) versus ultimate truth (paramattha), the friction remains undeniable within the canon's prose layer. The Aṭṭhakavagga is completely free from this theological problem, because it bypasses these rigid, later prose formulations entirely, focusing solely on immediate psychological peace.</p>
