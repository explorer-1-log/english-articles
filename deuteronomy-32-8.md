<p><br />In contemporary biblical scholarship, readers and researchers are heavily conditioned by the "Council of the Gods" theory when approaching Deuteronomy 32:8. This popular academic consensus claims that the Most High divided the nations of the world among lesser deities according to the number of the "sons of God" (bene Elohim). Such an interpretation relies almost entirely on southern textual traditions—specifically the Dead Sea Scrolls (4QDeutj) and the Greek Septuagint—which are then retrojected onto Ancient Near Eastern (ANE) mythologies like the Ugaritic texts.</p>
<p><br />However, if we strip away external traditions, later writings (such as the Nevi'im and Ketuvim), and modern ANE comparative mythology, and instead read the Torah through a <b><span style="font-size: medium;">strict literal lens</span></b> anchored in the northern tradition of the <b><span style="font-size: medium;">Samaritan Pentateuch</span></b>, the "Council of the Gods" theory collapses entirely. The Samaritan Torah is a self-contained textual system that requires no pagan mythological crutches to make sense.</p><p><br /></p>
<h2 style="text-align: left;">1. The Core Textual Divide: Bene Yisrael vs. Bene Elohim</h2>
<p><br />In the Samaritan Pentateuch, Deuteronomy 32:8 explicitly and literally reads l'mispar bene Yisrael—"according to the number of the children of Israel." While this aligns with the Masoretic Text (MT), it stands in sharp contrast to Qumran's bene Elohim ("sons of God/gods") and the Septuagint's aggelon theou ("angels of God").</p>
<p><br />When reading the Samaritan Pentateuch as a closed, self-interpreting unit, there is zero textual basis for introducing subordinate deities or angelic rulers over the nations. The bene Elohim variant from southern manuscripts reflects a late theological bias and scribal interpolation from the Second Temple period, an era when angelology and cosmic hierarchies were heavily absorbed into southern Jewish literature.</p><p><br /></p>
<h2 style="text-align: left;">2. Who Are the "Goyim" (Nations) in Deuteronomy 32:8?</h2>
<p><br />The greatest blind spot in modern academic scholarship is its obsessive fixation on the bene Yisrael versus bene Elohim debate, while completely overlooking the identity of the <b><span style="font-size: medium;">goyim</span></b> ("nations") within the very same verse. Deuteronomy 32:8 states that the Most High gave the goyim their inheritance and set their boundaries according to the number of the children of Israel.</p>
<p><br />If we allow the Torah to define its own terms internally, the concept of goyim follows a clear, literal trajectory starting in Genesis:</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Genesis 10 (The Origin of Physical Nations):</span></b> Following the flood, the descendants of Noah’s sons—<b><span style="font-size: medium;">Yafets, 'Am, and Shem</span></b> (following authentic Samaritan oral pronunciation)—branched out to form the goyim across the earth. Specifically through the lineage of 'Am and his son Canaan, the Torah already establishes the geographical boundaries of the territories occupied by these nations.</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Genesis 48:19 (Ephraim as the Fullness of Nations)</span></b>: When Jacob blesses Ephraim, the text uses the exact same root term: melo ha-goyim, declaring that Ephraim’s descendants will become the "fullness of nations/gentiles." Within the covenantal framework of the Torah, goyim is not merely a blanket term for pagan outsiders; it is directly tied to the territorial inheritance and multiplication of Israel's own lineage—specifically Ephraim, the ancestral cornerstone of the northern Samaritan tradition.</p><p><br /></p>
<h2 style="text-align: left;">3. The Smoking Gun: Benyamim Tsedaka’s Note and Genesis 15:18–21</h2>
<p><br />The most decisive blow to the "Council of the Gods" theory comes from the marginal notes provided by Israelite Samaritan scholar Benyamim Tsedaka in The Israelite Samaritan Version of the Torah. In his commentary on Deuteronomy 32:8, Tsedaka notes that "the number of the children of Israel" (bene Yisrael) refers specifically to the number <b><span style="font-size: medium;">12</span></b> (the twelve tribes of Israel), and he cross-references this verse directly to <b><span style="font-size: medium;">Genesis 15:18–21</span></b>.</p>
<p><br />Turning back to Genesis 15:18–21, we find YHWH cutting a covenant to grant the land of inheritance to Abram’s descendants—a land then inhabited by specific nations (goyim). In the Samaritan Pentateuch (following Samaritan vocalization), the text lists these nations as follows:</p>
<p><br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Verse 19</span></b>: Qinee, Qehnazee, and Qadamonee (3 nations).<br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Verse 20</span></b>: Ihtee, Ferizee, and Rehfa'ee (3 nations).<br /><b><span style="font-size: medium;">• Verse 21</span></b>: Ehmaree, Kaananee, Giegeshee, <b><span style="font-size: medium;">Ibbiee</span></b>, and Yebosee (5 nations).</p>
<p><br />Notice the crucial presence of the <b><span style="font-size: medium;">Ibbiee</span></b> (the Hivites) in verse 21 of the Samaritan Pentateuch—a name that was dropped or lost in the Masoretic Text of Genesis 15:21. Preserving the Ibbiee in the Samaritan text is vital: it maintains the original structural count of the Canaanite nations (mirroring the 11 Canaanite branches in Genesis 10 and the territorial span from the River of Egypt to the Euphrates) so that the land allocation aligns with the <b><span style="font-size: medium;">12 tribes of the children of Israel</span></b> destined to inherit and divide it.</p><p><br /></p>
<h2 style="text-align: left;">4. Putting the Literal Pieces Together</h2>
<p><br />When we connect these internal links from Genesis to Deuteronomy, the meaning of Deuteronomy 32:8 becomes crystal clear without any mythological gymnastics:</p>
<p><br />1. From the post-flood dispersion (Genesis 10) to the Abrahamic land covenant (Genesis 15:18–21), God fixed the geographical boundaries of the nations (goyim) occupying the land of Canaan.</p>
<p><br />2. The territorial expanse of these original nations (including the Ibbiee preserved in the Samaritan text) was measured and predetermined from the beginning to accommodate and be divided among the <b><span style="font-size: medium;">12 tribes of the children of Israel</span></b> (l'mispar bene Yisrael).</p>
<p><br />3. Jacob’s descendants—spearheaded by Ephraim, blessed to become melo ha-goyim (Genesis 48:19)—are the rightful heirs who step into those exact territorial boundaries (Deuteronomy 32:9).</p><p><br /></p>
<h2 style="text-align: left;">Conclusion</h2>
<p><br />The "Council of the Gods" theory forced upon Deuteronomy 32:8 is the byproduct of a flawed methodology that abandons the Torah’s internal consistency in favor of Ancient Near Eastern pagan parallels and late southern manuscript traditions. By reading the <b><span style="font-size: medium;">Samaritan Pentateuch</span></b> strictly and literally—tracing the lineage of Yafets, 'Am, and Shem in Genesis 10, the land covenant and the preserved Ibbiee list in Genesis 15:18–21, Ephraim’s blessing in Genesis 48:19, and the traditional count of the 12 tribes in Deuteronomy 32:8—it becomes undeniable that this passage is about <b><span style="font-size: medium;">the historical and geographical allocation of the Promised Land</span></b>, not a cosmic division of the world among lesser gods.</p>
