Preamble: The True Nature of the Words <em>Man</em> (مَنْ) and <em>Ma</em> (مَا) in the Quran</strong></h2><div><strong><br /></strong></div>
<div></div>
<div></div>
<p><strong>The Traditional Dogma</strong></p>
<p></p>
<div>Traditional rules often dictate that <em>Man</em> is exclusively reserved for sentient, rational entities (humans, angels, and jinn), while <em>Ma</em> is used strictly for non-rational objects.</div><div><br /></div><div><br /></div>
<div>
<p><strong>The Reality of the Quranic Text</strong></p>
<p></p>
In reality, the textual application of these two words is remarkably flexible. They frequently interchange depending on the specific perspective and context of the verse.</div>
<div>
<p><strong><br /></strong></p><p><strong><br /></strong></p><p><strong>Textual Evidence for Man</strong></p>
<p></p>
Surah An-Nur (24:45) employs <em>man</em> to describe creeping animals and four-legged beasts (<em>fa-minhum man yamshi</em>), showing it goes beyond just human or angelic intellect.</div>
<div>
<p><strong><br /></strong></p><p><strong><br /></strong></p><p><strong>Textual Evidence for Ma (First Instance)</strong></p>
<p></p>
Surah Al-Baqarah (2:228) uses <em>ma</em> to refer to the fetus inside the womb (<em>ma khalaqallahu fi arhamihinn</em>).</div><div><br /></div>
<div>
<p><strong><br /></strong></p><p><strong>Textual Evidence for Ma (Second Instance)</strong></p>
<p></p>
Surah Al-A'raf (7:190) uses <em>ma</em> for a human child who has already been born (<em>fima atahuma</em>), framing the child through the lens of a "bestowed object or gift."</div><div><br /></div>
<h2><strong>2. The Biological Status &amp; Organic Life of the Fetus in the Womb</strong></h2><div><strong><br /></strong></div>
<div></div>
<div></div>
<div>
<p><strong>An Organic Life</strong></p>
<p></p>
The fetus is a thriving, living entity—not a passive or lifeless biological object.</div><div><br /></div><div><br /></div>
<p><strong>The Contents of the Womb</strong></p>
<p></p>
<div>The Quran maintains the label <em>ma</em> (object) in verse 2:228 purely due to spatial location—because it is physically contained within the mother's womb—not because it lacks life.</div><div><br /></div>
<h2><strong>3. The Reality of the Word <em>Nafs</em> (نَفْس) vs. Foreign Concepts</strong></h2><div><strong><br /></strong></div>
<div></div>
<div></div>
<div>
<p><strong>Theological Misconceptions</strong></p>
<p></p>
<div>Many mistakenly translate <em>nafs</em> as "soul" (rooted in Greek philosophy), "atman" (from the Upanishads), or "atta" (from the Sutta Pitaka), imagining it as a sort of spiritual vapor that moves in and out of the physical body.</div><div><br /></div><div><br /></div>
</div>
<p><strong>The Quranic Literal Definition</strong></p>
<p></p>
<div>Linguistically, <em>nafs</em> is a universal term in the Quran meaning the "Self" or the "Existential Identity of an Entity."</div><div><br /></div><div><br /></div>
<div>
<p><strong>Textual Flexibility</strong></p>
<p></p>
The word <em>nafs</em> is applied to Allah (6:12 - Himself), human beings, the Jinn (6:130), and ironically, even to structured entities with physical components (7:195-197) to highlight their failure to defend their own 'physical selves' (anfusahum).</div><div><br /></div><div><br /></div>
<div>
<p><strong>The Semantic Status of Angels &amp; Animals</strong></p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div>
<div>While the word nafs is never explicitly paired with angels or animals in the text—<strong>as far as I am currently aware, subject to updates should new textual data emerge</strong>—logically and semantically, they must possess a nafs (a physical/existential identity) as they are structured, existing entities. This is anchored in the Quranic ironical application of anfusahum to structured idols that possess physical components like hands, feet, and eyes, yet fail to defend their own physical selves (7:195-197). Therefore, the apparent absence of explicit textual data for angels and animals is not a denial of their nafs. Rather, because the text heavily focuses the operational consequence of nafs on moral accounting (74:38), the Quran simply bypasses the physical identities of entities that lack free will and legal obligations under Divine Law.</div><div><br /></div><div><br /></div>
</div>
<div>
<p><strong>The Litmus Test of Verse 74:38</strong></p>
<p></p>
The phrase <em>kullu nafsin rahinah</em> ("every self is held in pledge") <strong>naturally excludes Allah</strong> because it is bound by the condition <em>bi ma kasabat</em> ("for what it has earned").&nbsp;While the word nafs is applied ironically to structured entities/idols to highlight their failure to defend their own <strong>physical selves</strong> (anfusahum), the consequence of being "held in pledge" <strong>applies solely to humans and jinn</strong> who bear moral and religious accountability.</div><div><br /></div>
<h2><strong>4. Criminal Law Context (Capital Punishment) &amp; Morality</strong></h2><div><strong><br /></strong></div>
<div></div>
<div></div>
<div>
<p><strong>The Definition of Nafs in Criminal Matters</strong></p>
In Surah Al-An'am (6:151), the prohibition against killing a <em>nafs</em> translates to the unlawful termination of a human life or physical body. However, the clause <em>illa bil-haqq</em> ("except by right of law") validates legitimate capital punishment mandated by a proper court of law.</div><div><br /></div><div><br /></div>
<div>
<p><strong>The Morality of Al-Maw'udah (81:8)</strong></p>
<p></p>
The discourse surrounding the infant girl buried alive (<em>al-ma'udah</em>) showcases the Quran's high moral standards. The vulnerable victim is given a "voice" in the afterlife to demand justice. This completely dismantles the civic logic of the <em>Jahiliyyah</em> (Pre-Islamic period), which refused to recognize infants as legal individuals.</div><div><br /></div>
<h2><strong>5. A Methodological Critique of the Pro-Choice Arguments</strong></h2><div><strong><br /></strong></div>
<div>
<p><strong>The Absolute Reality of Divine Design</strong></p>
<p></p>
The emergence of life within the womb occurs purely under divine design and execution. It does not subject itself to human autonomy, nor does it depend on the choice of the fetus itself.</div><div><br /></div><div><br /></div>
<div>
<p><strong>The Methodological Flaw in the Pro-Choice Reading</strong></p>
<p></p>
Leveraging text-based arguments to support a pro-choice stance for general cases (such as avoiding social consequences or economic anxieties) results in a reductive interpretation. It creates a self-serving conclusion that overlooks the textual rights and inherent status of the innocent fetus.</div><div><br /></div><div><br /></div>
<p><strong>Contextual Misapplication</strong></p>
<p></p>
<div>The attempt by certain arguments to justify abortion in rape cases is a clear contextual error. It cannot be methodologically extracted or stretched to legitimize the termination of an actively developing life.</div><div><br /></div>
<div>
<h2>6. Final Conclusion (Objective Morality)</h2>
While life presents real suffering and trauma—such as in cases of criminal rape—the textual framework of the Quran does not grant human reason the moral authority to terminate an innocent fetus. The principles of Quranic morality remain absolute and cannot be bypassed by pragmatic human convenience or selective textual readings.</div><div><br /></div>
<div></div>
<h2>7. Core Notes</h2>
<div>Reducing a fetus to a "lifeless object" based on the linguistic label Ma is a severe intellectual failure. The term is used strictly as a spatial descriptor of location or to frame a child as a bestowed divine gift—not as a denial of biological existence. Weaponizing grammar to justify abortion is entirely groundless.</div>
